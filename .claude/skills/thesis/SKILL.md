# Thesis

A piece makes one claim. This skill finds it, tests it against the research, and writes `brief.md` (shape: `templates/brief.md`). Read `lessons.md` first, then the genre profile and `piece.yaml`.

## 1. Start from the research, not from a blank page

- If the research note has a Stage 5 thesis (`## Thesis` or `### Thesis` in the passage or topic note), start there. If it has only a meaning statement (E10), start from that.
- If neither exists, the piece is not ready: run the write skill's research check now (stage 2) to file a request, set the piece to `waiting on research#N`, and stop.
- Copy the research's thesis into the brief with its file and line, then adapt it. Never restate it from memory.

## 2. Fit the claim to the genre

**Academic** (seminary paper, journal article, academic essay). Answer Kibbe's four questions [@kibbeTopicThesisGuide2016, 77–85] in the brief:
1. The specific issue.
2. The current conversation: the positions and who holds them (from the research positions map, by row).
3. My argument, in one or two sentences a reviewer could disagree with.
4. How it fits: what it adds, corrects or confirms.
Then the argument map: the claims in order, each with its evidence (research line), its strongest objection, and the answer. State what is out of scope and name the key source on it.

**Popular** (blog, counseling resource, popular book chapter). Write the **big idea** in one sentence a church member could repeat: what the passage is talking about (subject) and what it says about it (complement), in Haddon Robinson's terms. Then:
- the reader's question or need the big idea answers (from the reader line in `piece.yaml`);
- what the reader should believe and do afterward;
- the one claim most likely to be misread or resisted, flagged for the outline's explanation move.

## 3. Test it (both kinds)

1. **Certainty and time-frame.** Every clause of the thesis is no stronger than its research label: a *plausible* step is hedged or left out, never stated as certain, and promises keep their tense. Mark each clause with its label in the brief.
2. **Whole text.** Does the claim fit the whole passage, including verses the piece won't discuss? Name any it strains against.
3. **Doctrine.** Check the register entries the research lists for the passage (e.g. H17, H18). Write a short **Things not to say** list: wordings the research or register rules out, and tempting popular moves the text doesn't make (the 1 Peter brief listed "Peter the rock writes about stones," "capstone," reprobation stated as proven).
4. **Objection** (academic): the strongest objection from the positions map, in its holder's words, and the answer. If there is no answer, change the thesis.
5. **One sentence.** If the claim needs "and" to join two unrelated ideas, it is two pieces. Choose one.

## 4. Write the brief and stop at the checkpoint

Write `brief.md`, commit, and stop at the **thesis checkpoint** (write skill, "Checkpoints"): show Jack the claim with its certainty labels and the one or two decisions he most needs to weigh. Nothing is outlined until he approves, attended or not. If he edits `brief.md` himself, his wording stands; check it against the research and raise conflicts as questions.

Model: Opus. This is the step the piece stands or falls on.
