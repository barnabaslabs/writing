Few things wound like being left out on purpose. The invitation that never comes. The conversation that stops when you walk in. The joke told just loud enough for you to hear.

The Christians Peter wrote to knew that feeling well. They lived in five provinces of what is now Turkey, and most of them had grown up outside Israel, worshiping idols. When they came to Christ, they stopped joining their old friends in the idol worship and the drinking parties. Peter describes the reaction: "they are surprised that you do not run with them into the same excesses of dissipation, maligning you" (1 Pet 4:4). They were slandered and pushed to the edges of their own towns.

What would hold people like that steady? Peter does not start with advice. He starts with a description. In 1 Peter 2:4–10 he tells them who Jesus is, and then who they are.

## The stone people threw away

Peter calls Jesus "a living stone, rejected by people but in God's sight chosen and precious" (2:4).

He is drawing on Psalm 118:22, which he quotes a few lines later: "The stone that the builders rejected, this one has become the head of the corner" (2:7). Builders look over a stone, judge it useless and toss it aside. Then God takes that same stone and gives it the place of honor in the building.

Jesus applied that verse to himself (Mark 12:10). After the resurrection, Peter preached it to the council that had condemned Jesus (Acts 4:11). But in this letter Peter widens the circle. The builders are not one group; they are "people," everyone who looks at Jesus and decides he is not worth building on. That includes the neighbors who were mocking Peter's readers.

The cross was the builders' verdict. The empty tomb was God's.

Everything else in the passage depends on this. What God did with the rejected stone, he does with everyone who comes to it.

## Living stones in God's house

"Coming to him … you yourselves also, as living stones, are being built up as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (2:4–5).

Notice who does the building. Peter does not say "build yourselves." He says "you are being built." God is the builder. Believers are the stones.

Notice what he is building. A house where priests offer sacrifices is a temple. In Israel, priests were the ones who came near to God and served him on behalf of others. In this temple, the whole people of God is the priesthood. Their sacrifices are "spiritual," offered through Jesus Christ: the praise they give God and the good they do among their neighbors (2:9, 12).

A single stone lying in a field is just a rock. Set into a wall, it bears weight and holds up a roof. To come to Christ is to be set into his house, next to other believers, and given work to do there.

## Honor for those who believe

Next Peter quotes Isaiah 28:16, in the form he knew it: "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a cornerstone chosen and precious, and whoever believes in him will never be put to shame" (2:6).

Zion is Jerusalem, the city of God's temple. In Isaiah the stone is laid as a foundation, the cornerstone everything rests on. So when the psalm says the rejected stone became "the head of the corner," Peter means the chief stone at the foundation, not a stone set on top.

And the promise: never put to shame. Peter's readers were being shamed by their neighbors. He draws the conclusion plainly: "So the honor is for you who believe" (2:7). Their neighbors withheld honor. God gives it, now and on the last day, when he vindicates those who trusted his Son.

## Stumbling for those who refuse

The same stone does something else to those who refuse it. Quoting Isaiah 8:14, Peter calls it "a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense." They stumble, he says, "because they disobey the word; to this stumbling they were appointed" (2:8).

That last line is hard, so read it carefully. Peter does not say God forces anyone to disbelieve. He gives the cause: they disobey the word. What he says God appointed is the result. The God who laid the stone also settled what happens to those who reject it. The stone does not move. People either build on it or trip over it.

## What God calls you

Then Peter turns from the stumbling to his readers: "But you are an elect race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession" (2:9). "Elect" means chosen. The same word described Jesus a few verses earlier: he is God's chosen stone, and those who come to him are God's chosen people.

Every one of these titles comes from the Old Testament. At Mount Sinai God told Israel, "you shall be to Me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6). In Isaiah he spoke of "My chosen people, the people whom I formed for Myself" who "will recount My praise" (Isa 43:20–21).

Then Peter reaches for Hosea. When Israel had turned away, God told Hosea to name his son Lo-ammi, "for you are not My people" (Hos 1:9). Peter takes that language and uses it to tell his readers' own story: "Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy" (2:10).

So Peter describes these believers, most of them Gentiles, in the very words God first spoke to Israel. Two cautions keep us honest. Peter does not call them Israel, and he does not say Israel has lost what God promised it. Other Scriptures say God keeps his word to Israel (Rom 11:1–2, 28–29). And what Gentile believers have received is mercy. It is a gift, not a reason to look down on the people who heard these words first (Rom 11:18).

Why did God make them his people? "So that you may proclaim the excellencies of the one who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (2:9). A royal priesthood exists to tell what God has done.

## Living as God's chosen stones

What does this mean for a believer who is being passed over today?

First, God's verdict outranks the crowd's. People rejected Jesus, and God honored him. If you have come to him, God calls you chosen too, and the honor is yours. You do not need to win back the approval of people who withhold it. Peter's next words tell you to keep living honorably among them anyway (2:11–12).

Second, your life with the church is part of this priestly work. When believers praise God together and do good where they live, they offer what Peter calls "spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ."

Third, you have something to tell. God called you out of darkness into his marvelous light. Somebody near you needs to hear how he did it.

You may be the stone other people pass over. So was he. And God made him the stone the whole house rests on.
