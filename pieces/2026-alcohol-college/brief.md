# Brief

## Reader
A Christian college student, 18–22, deciding what to do about drinking. Some are under 21. Some are 21 or older but have signed a school code that forbids alcohol while enrolled. Named example (Jack, 2026-11-13): residential students at Liberty University live under an honor code that prohibits alcohol whatever the student's age. All will be free adults within a few years. Many have heard either "drinking is a sin" or "the Bible is fine with it" and assume those are the only two options. No Greek, no seminary; reads on a phone.

## Claim
Research thesis (barnabaslabs/research `topics/alcohol.md`, "Stage 5. Thesis," Kibbe question 3, revised after the review gates, commit 9a7d5d2): Scripture calls wine God's good gift and drunkenness sin, so the student's question is not whether alcohol is evil but whom he is answering to now: under the legal age, obeying the law is obeying Christ; under a code he signed, keeping his word is how he honors Christ; and Proverbs' counsel to the young adds a wisdom reason to stay clear. When he is free, faithfulness means enjoying the gift, if at all, under the Spirit's control and for others' good, never under the drink's, and abstaining remains a wise and honorable choice.

**Big idea (subject + complement):** The Bible's question about alcohol is not "Is it evil?" but "Whom are you answering to?": right now that means obeying the law, keeping your word, and heeding wisdom's warning to the young; later it means never letting drink, rather than the Spirit, rule you.

## Popular only
- **Reader's question or need:** "Is drinking a sin? If it isn't, why shouldn't I?" Behind it: "Are the rules I'm under just legalism?"
- **Believe afterward:** Alcohol is not evil, and obedience now is not legalism; it is how I honor Christ under the authorities and promises he has placed me in. Drunkenness is never an option for a Christian.
- **Do afterward:** Don't drink until the law allows it, and don't drink while under a code you agreed to keep. Take Proverbs' warning to the young seriously. Expect that friends may find it strange (1 Pet 4:4) and don't apologize for it. Later, if you drink, never to drunkenness, never to escape, never where it harms a brother, and only with a clear conscience; and know that choosing not to drink is wise and honorable.
- **Claim most likely to be misread or resisted:** "Drinking alcohol is not in itself sin." Some readers will hear it as permission or as an attack on the code. The outline needs an explanation move that shows the texts (Ps 104:15; Luke 7:33–34) and immediately ties the code to keeping one's word, not to the morality of drinking. Second: "a school code binds you" will be resisted by 21+ students; explain it from "let your yes be yes" (Matt 5:37) and say plainly it is an application, not a verse about school codes.

## Things not to say
- "Biblical wine was grape juice," "it was watered down," or "it was much weaker than ours." (Concordance and HALOT don't support the syrup/etymology claims; dilution unverified until Dunne is read.)
- "The Bible forbids drinking" or "Christians should never drink." (Commands to abstain are tied to roles, vows, callings or a command; Prov 23:31's scope is disputed.)
- "The Bible allows it, so the school rule doesn't matter." (Matt 5:37.)
- The code as God's own law or a direct command of Scripture. (Derived; Rom 14 warns against binding consciences.)
- "Peter says submit to every human institution," applied to a school. (Schreiner: Peter means governing authorities.)
- Jer 35 as the ground for keeping a code. (It is a father's command; use it, if at all, as an illustration.)
- "MacArthur ignores…" or any dismissive line about View B. (He grants the priests' rule may have been on duty and the vow voluntary; if the post touches the "royal priesthood" argument, answer it with 1 Pet 2:5, 9 and Luke 7:33–34, respectfully.)
- That one night of drunkenness proves someone was never saved. (The vice lists describe a pattern of life.)
- That Jesus supplied wine for people to get drunk. (John 2:10 describes a custom, not the guests or Jesus's purpose.)
- Anything from Dunne or the NotebookLM summary until the book is read with page numbers; the temperance-movement history as an argument against abstainers.
- Statistics or specific state laws unless a current source is cited ("what the law forbids you" is enough).
- Scolding absolutes ("no serious Christian drinks," "every drink is a step toward addiction").
- A personal story in Jack's voice he has not told. (Illustrations bank is empty; ask Jack.)
